AI-built websites all look the same: rounded cards, little status badges, the same soft shadows. The aesthetic has become a joke. But the sameness is a clue: **robots have preferences.**

In February 2026, [Amplifying studied 2,430 Claude Code responses](https://amplifying.ai/research/claude-code-picks) across 20 technology categories. The prompts didn't name products. They simply asked the robot to solve problems.

The results:

- shadcn/ui: 90% of UI component selections.
- Tailwind CSS: 68% of styling selections.
- Stripe: 91% of payment selections.
- Resend: 63% of email selections.
- Zustand: 65% of state management selections.
- Vitest: 59% of testing selections.

Meanwhile, Redux received zero selections in 88 state-management decisions. Jest was selected in just 4% of testing decisions.

The robots knew the incumbents. They preferred something else.

It's just a single study of Claude Code's choices, and doesn't prove real-world adoption. But it reveals the potential of a powerful new distribution channel.

## From ten blue links to one decision

Google spent years earning our trust by consistently delivering good search results. That trust transferred to the websites it ranked. Appearing among the ten blue links conferred authority; being the first result became an endorsement.

AI agents take this a step further: they can act on their own recommendations.

Ask a robot to build an app and it chooses the technologies, installs the dependencies, writes the code, and delivers something that works.

Sometimes those decisions never reach the operator. Other times, a less technical person sees the choices and says "lgtm 👍".

Either way, the robot has made the decision.

And that changes distribution. Google handed you a visitor. **A robot can hand you a customer.** Discovery, selection, and adoption can all happen within a single workflow.

## What makes a robot confident?

I suspect the underlying mechanism is *machine confidence*: how certain a robot can be that choosing a product will lead to a working result.

It isn't popularity. [Jest leads Vitest in reported usage in the 2025 State of JavaScript survey](https://2025.stateofjs.com/en-US/libraries/testing/), yet the robots overwhelmingly preferred Vitest. Incumbency doesn't protect you. Fit does.

Every working implementation adds another example for future models, so what the robot picks becomes what the robot learns from.

The strategy isn't radically different from SEO. What's good for humans and good for Google is generally good for robots.

But the tactics change when the ambition becomes: Get *this robot* to choose my product for *this class* of problem.

As robots take on more of the work, their preferences increasingly determine which products get used.
