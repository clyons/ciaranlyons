A store owner tells an AI: text anyone who leaves my store with something in their cart.

The agent compares SMS providers, picks one, and sets up an abandoned-cart flow for customers who've opted in.

A founder with a logo they love asks an AI to make 500 stickers from it. The agent picks a supplier with free shipping, places the order, and requests the founder's approval for the proof before production begins.

Neither customer needed to open a search engine or compare vendor websites.

Most advice about AI visibility is about getting mentioned. This is about getting picked, and what you can do about it.

I'll use two products as examples: SMS marketing software and stickers. Both are close to me. I'm a Postscript customer, and I've worked with Sticker Mule.

## 1. Pick the job

"Marketing software" is a category. "Bring shoppers back to complete their purchases" is a job.

"Stickers" is a category. "Make 500 stickers from my logo" is a job.

Choose the jobs you solve exceptionally well, in the words customers actually use. Then ask: when someone hands a robot this job, should it choose you?

## 2. Find out who the robots pick

Write 20 versions of the job without your brand name, varying the wording and customer context. Run them through the robots your buyers use, each one separately.

Record who gets recommended, who gets mentioned as an alternative, and how often the robot simply does the work itself. That last number is a competitor too.

Repeat the test. You need a baseline before you can change the result.

## 3. Say it in one sentence

State what you do, who it's for, and why you, in the customer's words. Make that proposition clear and consistent wherever a robot might encounter it.

If a salesperson can't explain your product in two sentences, don't expect a robot to get it right.

## 4. Earn proof beyond your own website

Independent reviews, customer discussions, case studies, and credible recognition all count. A genuine Reddit thread between customers may be worth more than a hundred company blog posts.

You can't manufacture authority with tricks. Ask happy customers to say so in public, publish outcomes with real numbers, and make that evidence easy to find.

## 5. Show the robot how to start

Knowing you exist isn't the same as knowing how you work. Provide [*machine confidence*](/writing/2026/11/01/becoming-the-robot-default/): give the robot reason to believe it will get the job done with your product.

An SMS company can publish setup guides for the abandoned-cart flow, make its APIs usable by agents, and show exactly how to get started. A sticker supplier can publish artwork requirements, proofing options, and how to place an order, written so a robot can follow them.

Every working example you publish is another one future models can learn from or retrieve.

## 6. Shorten the distance from yes to started

Count the steps between a robot choosing you and the job beginning. Remove every unnecessary form, approval, and handoff.

For SMS, that's a self-serve trial and transparent pricing. For stickers, it's an instant quote, free online proofs, and free shipping.

When human approval is required, make it a quick yes: a clear price, a clear summary of what happens next, and no surprises after the click.

## 7. Make success checkable

Give the robot a dependable way to know the job got done.

For SMS software, that's a compliant flow, test messages, and clear reporting on carts recovered. For stickers, it's proof approval, order status, and delivery confirmation.

Make the evidence easy for people and agents to check.

## 8. Keep testing

Run the original tests again. Are robots choosing you more often? Where are competitors still winning?

Then look beyond the recommendation. Did customers start? Did they finish the job? Did they get the result they wanted?

Improve the product, the public evidence, and the buying experience, then test again. As models change, their preferences change too. Nobody holds the default forever.

## The experiment

The interesting thing about this channel is that you can interrogate the decision-maker directly.

Give a robot your customer's job.

See who it chooses, what evidence it relies on, and where it gets stuck.

Make your product easier to understand, easier to trust, and easier to use.

Then give it the same job again.
